# 90-Day Social Strategy

## Positioning on LinkedIn

Dana is the validation engineer who got tired of watching paper eat weeks and built the rig she wished she had. Twelve years running protocols by hand, rigs in 30+ medtech plants, and she still runs the first pilot install personally. On LinkedIn, she says out loud what quality teams mutter in hallways: paper-based validation is a patient-safety risk, not an efficiency nuisance, and most QA automation vendors have never signed a deviation report in their lives. If a stranger read ten of her posts, they would say: this is the practitioner in a market full of vendors. Blunt about the tooling, warm to the people stuck using it, and armed with exact numbers instead of adjectives.

## Audience

Quality engineers, validation engineers, and VPs or Directors of Quality at 50-500 person medical device manufacturers. In their own words, their problem sounds like: "audit prep ate six weeks," "we printed 340 pages for one line change," "quality is the bottleneck for every product change." On LinkedIn they mostly lurk. They read posts from regulatory consultants, FDA-inspection commentary, and other quality people telling war stories, and they come alive in the comments when someone describes their exact Tuesday. The plan's working assumption: the validation engineer finds the post, the VP of Quality approves the demo, so every post should be worth forwarding up the chain.

## Content pillars

1. **The paper tax.** Scenes of what paper validation actually costs: pages printed, weeks lost, weekends burned. Business reason: it makes the pain undeniable and specific, and it is the post a validation engineer forwards to their VP. Those forwards are where demo conversations start.

2. **Practitioner's eye.** What 12 years of running protocols by hand teaches you that vendor demos never show. Business reason: the demo decision is a trust decision, and Dana's edge over vendors who have never run a protocol is the whole reason to pick Meridian. This pillar is that edge, in public, weekly.

3. **The audit question.** Automated evidence trails versus binders, and what auditors actually accept. Business reason: "will the FDA take this?" is the objection that stalls demos from booking. Answering it in public, repeatedly, shortens the path from interested to scheduled.

4. **Inside a pilot.** What actually happens when a rig lands on one production line, told by the founder who installs it herself. Business reason: it shows the demo-to-pilot-to-contract path is small and low-risk, which is what turns a curious commenter into a booked demo.

## Cadence

The budget is 45 minutes a week, so the math has to close at 45, not at what we wish we had.

- One post per week: 25-30 minutes. (Working heuristic: AI drafts it, Dana edits it into her voice. The edit is where the voice lives, so it does not get skipped.)
- One commenting session per week: 15 minutes of genuine comments on posts written by quality people and prospects. Manual, at human volume, no tools.

Total: 40-45 minutes. That is the whole week.

Two posts a week would cost 50-60 minutes before any commenting, which is over budget. An over-budget cadence fails by week 4 and takes the strategy's credibility with it, so it is one post, every week, for 13 weeks.

Once a month, the metrics tally (see "What we measure") costs 10 minutes. In that week, the commenting session shrinks to 5 minutes so the week still sums to 45: post (30) + tally (10) + comments (5). A plan that quietly runs over once a month is a plan that lied about its price.

## Channel plan

LinkedIn only, on Dana's personal profile. The buyers (quality engineers and VPs of Quality at medtech manufacturers) are on LinkedIn, and they reply to people, not logos. No secondary channel this quarter: splitting a 45-minute budget across two channels means losing on both. If the company page wants content, it can repost Dana's posts, but nothing in this plan depends on it.

## What we measure

The quarter's goal is 8-10 qualified demos a month, and referrals currently deliver 2-3. LinkedIn's job is to become the second demo source that closes that gap. Nobody can honestly promise a demo count from 45 minutes a week, and this document will not. What it can do is track whether the channel is moving toward demos, in order:

1. **Conversations started with real buyers.** Substantive comments from ICP titles and inbound DMs received. This is the leading indicator for months 1-2.
2. **Profile-to-site clicks.** Views of Dana's profile from buyer titles, and clicks through to the Meridian site.
3. **Demos booked that trace to LinkedIn.** Add "how did you hear about us?" to the demo booking flow and ask it on every first call. By month 3, this is the number that matters.
4. **Pipeline Dana can trace to LinkedIn.** Demos that became pilots.

Tracking cost: about 10 minutes once a month, tallied in a simple note or spreadsheet, paid for inside the weekly budget per the Cadence section, never on top of it.

Explicitly ignored: follower count, raw impressions, and likes. None of them tells you whether demos are coming, and optimizing for them produces content for an algorithm instead of for the VP of Quality who approves the pilot.

## First two weeks, day by day

Week 1 (setup plus the first post, 45 minutes total):

- **Monday (15 min).** Rewrite the LinkedIn headline and About section so a VP of Quality knows in one read what Dana does and for whom (10 min). Then follow 15-20 people your buyers actually are: quality and validation engineers at 50-500 person medtech companies, plus a few regulatory voices they read (5 min). This makes your feed the room where your buyers talk.
- **Wednesday (20 min).** Draft, edit, and publish post #1 from The paper tax pillar. Open with a plant-floor scene and an exact number. Publish in the morning.
- **Friday (10 min).** Reply to every comment on post #1. Replying keeps the thread alive longer and is where conversations with buyers actually start. If a buyer commented, answer their point; do not pitch.

Week 2 (rhythm, 45 minutes total):

- **Monday (15 min).** Commenting session: find 3-5 posts by quality people or prospects and leave genuine, substantive comments. Say something only Dana could say. No links, no pitches.
- **Wednesday (25 min).** Draft, edit, and publish post #2 from the Practitioner's eye pillar.
- **Friday (5 min).** Reply to comments on post #2 and answer any DMs. If someone's comment sounds like a live problem, ask one plain question about their setup. That is a conversation started, which is the first metric.

From week 3 on, repeat week 2's shape: one post, one commenting session, replies. Rotate pillars so each one appears roughly every third week, with The paper tax appearing most often since it starts the most buyer conversations.
